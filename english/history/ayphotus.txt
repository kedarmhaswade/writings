Chapter 9 Slavery and Emancipation
- Slavery in the American South
  -- Harriet Tubman
- The Abolition Movement
  -- The abolitionists 
  -- The North had about 130,000 free blacks in 1830. Twenty years later there were 200,000. 
  -- Frederick Douglass was born into slavery, learned to read and write, and escaped into the North at the age of 21. 
  -- Douglass called "the idea of being a free man some day" a dream that "all the powers of slavery" could not destroy.

- Civil War and Slavery
  -- The North wanted their economy to expand.
  -- Lincoln sided with the North.
  -- When Lincoln became the president, seven Southern states seceded (left the union).
  -- The South formed the Confederacy and the Civil War was on.
  -- Lincoln went to war not to free slaves, but to bring the South back into the union-"If I could save the union without freeing any slave, I'd do it; and if I could save it by freeing all the slaves, I'd do it."
  -- Thirteenth amendment ended slavery.

- Emancipation without Freedom
  -- White violence against blacks erupted in the South almost as soon as the war ended.
  -- It was only a matter of time before blacks would be returned to a condition that was not far from slavery, even if they remained legally free. 

10. The Other Civil War
- The Myth of "Jacksonian Democracy"
- Big Business
  -- In the 1850s, the state and federal governments gave away 25 million acres to railroad companies, along with millions of dollars of loans.
  -- "On the eve of the Civil War, the men who ran the country were most concerned with money and profit, not the movement against slavery."
- Workers Unite
  -- Trade unions began to form as workers banded together to bargain for better pay and working conditions.
  -- The courts struck back at unions, calling them illegal conspiracies to hurt businesses. 
  -- The labor movement had started off well in Philadelphia. However, a religious rift between American-born Protestant workers and Irish immigrant Catholic weavers made it fall apart.
  -- The poor had no choice but get drafted into the Union army. Rich men could pay $300 to get out of serving. 

Rule ad Rebellion
- Railroad workers rebelled. A depression struck in the 1870s.
- Just as African Americans had learned that they did not have enough strength to make good promises of emancipation, working people learned that they were not united or strong enough to defeat the combination of private wealth and government power. But their fight would continue ...


11. Robber Barons and Rebels
- Between the Civil War and 1900, steam and electricity replaced human muscle. The US built 193,000 miles of railroads. Inventors and businesspeople caused 'progress.'
- Progress demanded labor. Much of the work was done by immigrants. On the west coast, Chinese immigrants made up 1/10th of the CA population in 1880.
- Chinese and Jewish newcomers became the targets of racial attacks, sometimes at the hands of those, such as Irish, who had immigrated earlier. (Insiders and Outsiders!)
- The Rich Get Richer
  -- Some (new American) multimillionaires started in poverty. Their 'rags to riches' stories were useful for making the masses of poor workers believe that they, too, could be wealthy someday. The great majority of millionaires, however, came from upper-class or middle-class families.
  -- Richest men of that era: J. P. Morgan, John D. Rockefeller, Andrew Carnegie, James Mellon, and Jay Gould.
  -- Mellon's father wrote to him, "There are plenty of lives less valuable [than yours]."
  -- These men and others built huge fortunes with the help of the government and the courts. (Thomas Edison, e.g., promised NJ politicians $1000 each if they would make laws to favor his business interests.)
  -- Rockefeller created an oil monopoly.
- Voices of Protest
  -- A few politicians tried to limit the power of corporations. One senator, John Sherman, wrote an Anti-trust Act, which congress voted into law in 1877. Sherman feared that without reforms, people who opposed the power of giant corporations might be drawn to dangerous new political ideas that had come out of Europe.
  -- One idea was Socialism--an economic system in which the government or the people as a whole own the means of production, such as farms, mines, and factories. These are operated for the benefit of all, not for private profit.
  -- Another idea, Communism, went even further, doing away with private property and with class distinctions based on wealth. In a Communist society, all goods would be owned by everyone, available to anyone according to need. 
  -- A third new political idea, anarchism, held that government itself was unnecessary, even wrong.
  -- The Sherman Anti-trust act was designed to reform the capitalist system just enough to prevent socialism or communism from taking hold among the workers and poor. Less than 20 years after it became law, however, the US supreme court interpreted the act in a way that made it meaningless. At the same time, the Court was giving added protection to corporations. These decisions kept wealth at the top of the pyramid. One supreme court justice, David J. Brewer, said in 1893, "It is the unvarying law that the wealth of the community will be in the hands of the few ..."
  -- Churches, schools, business, and government tried to control people's thinking, teaching them that all was right with the society. Poverty was a sign of personal failure. The rich deserved to be rich. The capitalist system was right and proper.
  -- Two counterexamples: Henry George's 1897 book, Progress and Poverty, and Edward Bellamy's 1888 book, Looking Backward (2000-1887). Bellamy painted a hopeful picture of the future and the society was socialistic: Everyone worked and lived in cooperation, not as competing individuals. Revolution was taking roots.
  -- The Haymarket Affair
  -- Debs's arrest: The issue was Socialism versus Capitalism. 
  -- Populism (the rise and fall of): This was the new movement that embodied the political and economic beliefs and activities of the people. Populists were against monopolies and capitalism. In the end, the populist movement failed to unite blacks and whites, farmers, and urban workers. 
  -- In the 1896 election, the corporations and the press threw their support behind the Republican, William McKinley. He turned to patriotism to drown the class resentment: "I'm glad to know that the people in every part of this country mean to be devoted to one flag, the glorious Stars and Stripes, the people of this country mean to maintain the financial honor of this country as sacredly as they maintain the honor of the flag."

12. The American Empire
- "I should welcome almost any war, for I think this country needs one." Those words were written in 1897, in a letter to a friend, by Theodore Roosevelt. 
- Before he was elected president, William McKinley said, "We want a foreign market for our surplus goods."
- Albert Beveridge said, "American factories are making more than the American people can use; American soil is producing more than they can consume. Fate has written our policy for us; the trade of the world must and shall be ours."
- War was probably not a thought-out plan among most of the elite ruling classes. Instead, it grew naturally from two sources, capitalism and nationalism. Capitalism demanded more markets. Nationalism, the spirit of strong national pride, made people think that the US had a right, or even a duty, to expand itself and to shape the affairs of other countries.

- The Taste of Empire
  -- The Monroe Doctrine, created by president James Monroe in 1823, made it clear to European nations that the Americas were not their, but the US's, business to meddle with!
  -- The US too active role in other countries affairs 103 times from 1798 to 1895.

- The Spanish-American War in Cuba
  -- President Grover Cleveland and the British politician, Winston Churchill, thought that Cuba might turn into another "black and white republic." Churchill also feared that Cuba may become Haiti, the only country in the Americas run by black people. (As of 2026, Haiti is described as a 'failed state' by Wikipedia. Columbus landed in Haiti on 6 December 1492.) 
  -- The United States defeated Spain, but nearly 5500 American soldiers died, most of them because of rotten, tainted meat provided by American meatpackers!
  -- Americans added 'Platt Amendment', that gave Americans control over Cuba.

- Revolt and Racism in the Philippines
  - The US did not annex Cuba (Annexation is a unilateral act where one country formally takes over another country's or territory's land and absorbs it into its own domain, claiming full sovereignty over it.)
  - The US, however, got control of Puerto Rico, Wake Island, Guam, and the large island cluster called the Philippines.
  - McKinley pushed for the Philippines war, but they revolted. The conflict lasted 3 years and the United States prevailed. The US would maintain control of the Philippines till about 1946. (This part is not very well described by Zinn.)

Part II

Class Struggle to the War on Terror

13. Class Struggle

Anger was on the rise in America as the 20th century opened.

- Emma Goldman, an anarchist and feminist of the time spoke against the Spanish-American war (Cuba).
- Some famous American writers spoke up for Socialism, with harsh words for the capitalist system. Jack London's novel, The Iron Heel, published in 1906, offered a socialist brotherhood of man. 

Sweatshops ad Wobblies
- Businesses were looking for ways to produce more goods and make more money.
- In NYC, many immigrants went to work in garment factories called sweatshops, where they worked for very low wages under unhealthy conditions.
- One of the NY's hundred sweatshops was the Triangle Shirtwest Company. Women workers there went on strike in the winter of 1909. ... The strike lasted for months against police, scabs, and arrests. Yet although the workers won some of their demands, conditions in the factory did not change much. In March 1911, a fire broke out in the Triangle building. (Wikipedia reports that the fire was the deadliest industrial disaster in the history of the city, and one of the deadliest in the U.S. history; 146 people--123 women and girls and 23 men--died. (The factory was in the Asch building, later Brown building, whose remains stand on the campus of the NYU! Because the doors to the stairwells and exits were locked[1][7]—a common practice at the time to prevent workers from taking unauthorized breaks and to reduce theft[8]—many of the workers could not escape from the burning building and jumped from the high windows. There were no sprinklers in the building.) 100,000 New Yorkers marched in their memorial parade.

- The union movement was growing, but the biggest union, the American Federation of Labor (AFL) did not represent all workers. (My update: There is still heavy industry in the U.S. in 2026, but I guess by making China the manufacturing headquarters on the world, the U.S. seems to have moved the "union problems" to China!) Its members were almost all white, male, skilled laborers. Blacks were kept out of the AFL. Women made up a fifth of the workforce in 1910, but only one in a hundred women workers was in a union. In addition, AFL officials had begun to seem no better than corporate bosses. They were protected by "goon squads" who beat up union members who criticized them.
- At a 1905 meeting of anarchists, socialists, and unionists in Chicago, a union was born. It was called the Industrial Workers of the World (IWW), and its goal was to organize all workers in any industry into "One Big Union," undivided by sex, race, or skills. The IWW came to be called Wobblies, though it's not clear why. They met force with force.
- The IWW was inspired by a new idea that was developing in Spain, Italy, and France. This was anarcho-syndicalism, the belief that workers could take power in a country, not by seizing control of the government in an armed rebellion, but by bringing the economic system to a halt.
- In the 10 exciting years after its birth, the IWW became a threat to the capitalist class in the US. Their ability to organize strikes and protests made a big impact on the country. IWW organizers were unemployed, or moved around as migrant workers. They sang, spoke, and spread message and their spirit.
