\chapter{Let's Count!}

\section{A Party}
This section introduces the art of counting gently, using a running example of seven friends partying at Alice's house on her birthday: Alice, Bob, Carl, Diane, Eve, Frank, and George. They initially sit at a round table and eat.

Counting problems can be daunting. 

My main challenges in solving counting problems:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Interpret the given problem correctly. This makes a combinatorial problem as a `word problem.' The language skill is critical.
    \item Think about a given counting problem in more than one equivalent way. 
\end{itemize}

Authors choose not to explicitly declare the multiplication principle which is useful many a time:
\begin{definition}[Multiplication Principle]
    \label{def:mp}
If one choice can be made in $m_1$ ways and another choice in $m_2$ ways, and the two choices are independent, then the two choices together can be made in $m_1\times m_2$ ways.

In general, if there are $n$ choices, the first one can be made in $m_1$ ways, second in $m_2$ ways, and so on, and the choices are independent, then the total number of ways in which those choices can be made together is $m_1\times m_2\times\dots\times m_n$.
\end{definition}

\begin{exercise}
    How many ways can these people be seated at the table if Alice, too, can sit anywhere?
    \label{ex:1.1.1}
\end{exercise}
\textbf{Solution}.

Since Alice can sit at one of the seven chairs, she can make the choice in seven ways. Once Alice makes a choice, Bob can choose any one of the remaining six seats. Is Bob's choice \emph{independent} of Alice's? Clearly, Bob can't choose the seat that Alice chose. However, regardless of that, Bob has six seats to choose from. The number of Bob's choices is independent of the choice Alice makes. From multiplication principle it follows that Alice and Bob can be seated in $7\times 6=42$ different ways. 

This process continues until George has the Hobson's choice of taking the only remaining seat when it's his turn to choose. It follows from the multiplication principle (See definition [\ref{def:mp}]) that the total number of ways these 7 friends can \emph{choose} 7 seats together is $7\times 6\times 5\times\dots\times 2\times 1=7!=5040$.

We have counted the number of ways a seating arrangement can be done, given the constraints, without listing the arrangements and enumerating them. Enumerating is one way of counting, but it is not the only way. Counting \emph{without} enumerating is a huge time-saver, provided our argument (based on the multiplication principle in this case) is correct. 

On the other hand, \emph{enumerating} such arrangements is also an important aspect of combinatorial problems. 

\subsection*{Pairing Chess Players}
$6$ friends ($2n$, in general) want to play chess on $3$ chessboards ($n$, in general). In how many ways can they be paired? Consider that the chessboards are numbers $1,2,3,\dots,n$.

Well, we must convey the constraints clearly. The book calls a set of these constraints `a sense of pairing' and names them after the player who suggests them. At least three pairing schemes are suggested:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Bob's sense (``I think you should not count it as a different pairing if two people at the same board switch places,'' says Bob, ``and it shouldn't matter which pair sits at which board.'')

    \item Carl's sense (``Yes, I think we have to agree on what the question really means,'' adds Carl. ``If we include in it who plays white on each board, then if a pair switches places we do get a different matching. But Bob is right that it doesn't matter which pair uses which board.'') 

    \item Diane's sense: ("What do you mean it does not matter? You sit at the first board, which is closest to the peanuts, and I sit at the last, which is farthest," says Diane.)
\end{enumerate}


\begin{exercise}
    What is the number of pairings in Carl's sense (when it matters who sits on which side of the board, but the boards are all alike), and in Diane's sense (when it is the other way around)?
    \label{ex:1.1.2}
\end{exercise}
\textbf{Solution}.

\begin{mybox}[Wrong Answers Only!]
The book calculates the number of pairings in Bob's sense: $15$. Indeed, as a game of chess is played between two opponents, the number of pairings is the same as the number of games played when any two players are chosen (with 6 choices for the first player, and having made that choice, 5 for the second) and they play only one game between them, although two are possible, for example, George (White) versus Carol (Black) and Carol (White) versus George (Black). The number of games is: 
\[
    \frac{6\times 5}{2}=15
\]

In Carl's sense, this number doubles. The Diane-Frank pairing is distinct from the Frank-Diane pairing. We always give the first player White pieces and the second player Black. Thus, every pair plays two games and there are $15$ pairs as above. Therefore, the number of pairings in Carl's sense is $30$.

In Diane's sense, we consider the Bob-Carol pairing on board 1 distinct from the Bob-Carol pairing on board 2 or board 3. It's like pairing them up on board 1 first ($15$ pairings, since sides don't matter), then on board 2 ($15$ more pairings), and lastly on board 3 (still $15$ more pairings) giving $45$ pairings.
    \label{box:wrong-1}
\end{mybox}

\begin{reflection}[I want to work harder]
    \label{ref:work-harder}
    My initial answer above (box [\ref{box:wrong-1}]) shows my gross misunderstanding of the problem. I had read the relevant section from the book and thought I had understood it, but alas, I hadn't! A serious discussion with my son, Apoorv Mhaswade, revealed it to me. I am very grateful to him! 

    This also means that passive reading is not enough. For serious study of known material, one must actively engage in quizzing oneself. One must experiment, solve problems, and, if solutions are provided, check them thoroughly. I thank Lov\'asz for providing the correct solutions. While studying by oneself, this can feel onerous, but there's no choice to get more familiar with a vast body of known technical matter. For new research, this means becoming more immune to confirmation bias.

    Combinatorial problems act as if they have a knack of fooling you! Wrong thinking about a concrete problem can lead to the correct answer, giving you a false confidence that you are right. My attempt above demonstrates that I completely misinterpreted the problem and each of the `senses' in which the counting is specified. This is frustrating, but it emphasizes the indispensability of hard work.


    First, we need to pair 6 players across 3 chessboards. This is different from 6 people shaking hands. The handshake custom doesn't care on which side of the handshake you are and, unlike the chessboard, there's no `handshake board.' 

    Alice initially thinks that the number of ways of pairing six people on three chessboards is equivalent to the number of ways of seating them on six chairs and placing a chessboard between each pair across it. If this is how want to pair them up, then let that be. The number of ways of seating six friends in six chairs simultaneously is clearly $6!=720$. Alice is right in her own right although each player may be paired with the same opponent on the same board.

    It's important to note (although not mentioned explicitly) that all the players sit and start playing their `round' simultaneously. It's \emph{not} that players finish playing on board 1 (while board 2 and board 3 are unused), then board 2, and finally board 3; in other words, they \emph{do not use the available boards sequentially}. 

    The arrangement initially proposed by Alice can also be realized another way: For the board 1, we can choose the first player in $6$ ways and the second player in $5$ ways, since sides matter (Bob with White pieces versus Carl with Black must be counted differently from Bob with Black versus Carl with White). Those are $6\times 5=30$ pairings. Once those two players occupy board 1, we only have $6-2=4$ choices for the first player on board 2 and $3$ for the second. That gives $4\times 3=12$ independent choices for board 2. Similarly, $2\times 1=2$ independent choices for board 3. Together (from Multiplication Principle (definition [\ref{def:mp}])), the total number of choices for the matching or pairing is
    \[
        30\times 12\times 2=720
    \]

    Listing all the pairs across three boards is mechanical.

    However, Bob thinks that once two are paired, they must not be paired again\footnote{To emphasize, they must not be paired again on any board.}. This means that the board 1 pair can only be chosen $5$ ways\footnote{\textbf{Not} $6\times 5=30$ as in Alice's scheme, where each pair plays many times on the same board.} (you choose a player and their opponent can be chosen from the remaining $5$, giving $5$ pairs); simultaneously, the board 2 pair can only be independently chosen $3$ ways; and the board 3 pair can be chosen independently once.

    Therefore, the total number of choices for pairing in Bob's sense is
    \[
        5\times 3\times 1=15
    \]
    This number of pairings is the same in any scenario. One scenario is listed below (table [\ref{tab:bob-sense-bob-b1}]).
\begin{table}[H] 
\centering
\begin{tabular}{|c|c|c|}
 \hline
 Board 1&Board 2&Board 3\\ \hline
 \hline
    Bob-Carl&Diane-Eve&Frank-George\\ \hline
    Bob-Diane&Carl-Frank&Eve-George\\ \hline
    Bob-Eve&Carl-George&Diane-Frank\\ \hline
    Bob-Frank&Carl-Eve&Diane-George\\ \hline
    Bob-George&Carl-Diane&Eve-Frank\\ \hline
\end{tabular}

\label{tab:bob-sense-bob-b1}
    \caption{A Scenario for Pairings in Bob's Sense (Bob \emph{Sticks} to Board 1)}
\end{table}

    In this scenario, Bob only plays on board 1. It's possible that Carl plays only on board 1, or Diane only plays on board 1, and so on. In each of those scenarios (there are 6), the \emph{number of pairings stays the same}, 15.

    Carl's wording ("If we include in it who plays white\dots") is arguably confusing. What I now think he means is that a Bob (White)-Carl (Black) pair on a given board is distinct from Bob (Black)-Carl (White) pair \emph{on the same board}, but not distinct on another board: A pair plays twice on a board; they don't play again on any other board.

    Thus, Carl wants to count each pair on the same board twice. Therefore, the total number of choices for pairing in Carl's sense is
    \[
       10\times 6\times 2=120 
    \], or equivalently,
    \[
        (5\times 2)\times(3\times 2)\times(1\times 2)=5\times 3\times 1\times 2^3=15\times 2^3=120
    \]

    Carl multiplies Bob's count by $2^n$.

    Diane's assertion is also tricky to state clearly. I think she wants to retain Bob's sense of not pairing the same players again on the same board, but not retain his sense of not pairing them on another board. Consider an arrangement of three boards in Bob's sense: Board 1: Bob-Carl, Board 2: Diane-Eve, Board 3: Frank-George. This 3-board arrangement is counted by Bob. However, Diane counts this and all its rearrangements (permutations) distinctly: 

\begin{table}[H] 
\centering
\begin{tabular}{|c|c|c|}
 \hline
 Board 1&Board 2&Board 3\\ \hline
 \hline
    Bob-Carl&Diane-Eve&Frank-George\\ \hline
    Bob-Carl&Frank-George&Diane-Eve\\ \hline
    Diane-Eve&Bob-Carl&Frank-George\\ \hline
    Diane-Eve&Frank-George&Bob-Carl\\ \hline
    Frank-George&Bob-Carl&Diane-Eve\\ \hline
    Frank-George&Diane-Eve&Bob-Carl\\ \hline
\end{tabular}

\label{tab:diane-sense}
\caption{Diane Counts What Bob and Carl Don't}
\end{table}

    These are the placements of 3 pairs in 3 places (across each chessboard). Their number is $3\times 2\times 1=3!=6$. Each such 3-pair from Bob's pairings is multiplied by this number by Diane. Therefore, in Diane's sense, the number of pairings is $15\times 3!=15\times 6=90$.

    Diane multiplies Bob's count by $n!$.

    It turns out that Alice's initial estimate, $(2n)!$, equals Bob's count ($(2n-1)\times(2n-3)\times\dots\times 3\times 1$) multiplied by Carl's factor ($2^{n}$) and Diane's factor ($n!$). 
\end{reflection}

    In Diane's sense, $945\times5\times4\times3\times2\times1=945\times 5!=\num{113400}$.

Interestingly, 
    \[

    \]
\end{reflection}

\section{Sets and the Like}
Sets are complicated, but here's an introduction. Sets are a fundamental tool that helps with counting problems.

\begin{definition}[Set]
Any collection of distinct objects, called elements, is a set.
    \label{def:set}
\end{definition}
\begin{definition}[Empty Set or Null Set]
    A set with no elements is called an Empty Set. It's denoted by $\emptyset$.
    \label{def:empty-set}
\end{definition}
\begin{definition}[Cardinality of a Set]
    \label{def:set-cardinality}
    The number of elements of a set is its cardinality.

    For example, the cardinality $\lvert\mathbb{P}\rvert$ of the set $\mathbb{P}=\{1,4,9,16\}$ is $5$.

    We'll not discuss infinite sets (sets whose cardinality is $\infty$) here.

\end{definition}
\begin{definition}[Subset]
    A set $\mathbb{A}$ is called a subset of set $\mathbb{B}$ if every element of $\mathbb{A}$ is an element of $\mathbb{B}$.

    In other words, $\mathbb{A}$ consists of certain elements of $\mathbb{B}$. 

    We can allow $\mathbb{A}$ to consist of all elements of $\mathbb{B}$ (in which case $\mathbb{A}=\mathbb{B}$) or none of them (in which case $\mathbb{A}=\emptyset$), and still consider it a subset. So the empty set is a subset of every set.

    This is denoted by $\mathbb{A}\subseteq\mathbb{B}$. If $\lvert\mathbb{A}\rvert\ne\lvert\mathbb{B}\rvert$, then $\mathbb{A}$ is called a \emph{proper subset} of $\mathbb{B}$, denoted by $\mathbb{A}\subset\mathbb{B}$.
    \label{def:subset}
\end{definition}

We consider operations on two sets.

\begin{definition}[Intersection]
    The intersection, $\mathbb{A}\cap\mathbb{B}$, of two sets, $\mathbb{A}$ and $\mathbb{B}$, is the set consisting of elements common to both the sets.

    When there are no common elements, this set is $\emptyset$, and the two sets are said to be \emph{disjoint} with each other.
    \label{def:set-intersection}
\end{definition}
\begin{definition}[Union]
    The union, $\mathbb{A}\cup\mathbb{B}$, of two sets, $\mathbb{A}$ and $\mathbb{B}$, is the set consisting of elements from either set.

    \label{def:set-union}
\end{definition}
\begin{definition}[Difference]
    The difference, $\mathbb{A}\setminus\mathbb{B}$, between two sets, $\mathbb{A}$ and $\mathbb{B}$, is the set consisting of elements from $\mathbb{A}$ but not $\mathbb{B}$.
    The difference, $\mathbb{B}\setminus\mathbb{A}$, between two sets, $\mathbb{B}$ and $\mathbb{A}$, is the set consisting of elements from $\mathbb{B}$ but not $\mathbb{A}$.

    \label{def:set-difference}
\end{definition}
\begin{definition}[Symmetric Difference]
    The symmetric difference, $\mathbb{A}\triangle\mathbb{B}$, between two sets, $\mathbb{A}$ and $\mathbb{B}$, is the set consisting of elements from exactly one of the two sets.

    \label{def:set-sym-difference}
\end{definition}

The exposition here brightly illuminates the relevant portion of set theory! The authors compare sets with natural numbers and draw parallels between them to the extent possible. They challenge readers to think of set intersection like multiplication (this may not be natural), set union like addition (quite natural), and the two set differences like subtraction (somewhat natural) of numbers. These similarities and differences beget some remarkable set identities that may reveal as a positive side-effect of solving the following exercises.

\begin{exercise}
    We form the union of a set $\mathbb{A}$ with $5$ elements and a set $\mathbb{B}$ with $9$ elements.  Which of the following numbers can we get as the cardinality of their union $\mathbb{U}$: $4$, $6$, $9$, $10$, $14$, $20$?
    \label{ex:1.2.12}
\end{exercise}
\textbf{Solution}. 
$\mathbb{U}$ must have at least $9$ elements because $\mathbb{B}$, which has $9$ elements, is its subset. Therefore, it couldn't have $4$ or $6$ elements. Similarly, it couldn't have $20$ elements; to get to that many elements, after we choose all the elements of $\mathbb{B}$, we have only $\mathbb{A}$ to choose from, which can only offer at most $5$ elements. In this case, $9$ (all the elements of $\mathbb{A}$ are also in $\mathbb{B}$; $\mathbb{A}\subset\mathbb{B}$), $10$ ($\mathbb{A}$ and $\mathbb{B}$ have four common elements) or $14$ ($\mathbb{A}$ and $\mathbb{B}$ are disjoint).

\begin{exercise}
    We form the union of two sets. We know that one of them has $n$ elements and the other has $m$ elements. What can we infer about the cardinality of their union?
    \label{ex:1.2.13}
\end{exercise}
\textbf{Solution}.
Two extremities as far as union $\mathbb{U}$ of two sets is concerned are 1) one of them is a subset of another, and 2) the two sets are disjoint. In 1), one of the sets (the subset) does not contribute new elements to the union. Therefore, in that case, the set with higher cardinality determines that of the union. In 2), since all elements from both the sets must be in the union, the union has $m+n$ elements; the union of any two sets cannot have a cardinality larger than that. $\therefore\max(m,n)\leq\lvert\mathbb{U}\rvert\leq(m+n)$.

\begin{proof}
    We reproduce the inequalities from the solutions to exercises [\ref{ex:1.2.13}] and [\ref{ex:1.2.15}].
    \begin{align*}
        \max(\lvert\mathbb{A}\rvert,\lvert\mathbb{B}\rvert)&\leq\lvert\mathbb{\mathbb{A}\cup\mathbb{B}}\rvert\leq(\lvert\mathbb{A}\rvert+\lvert\mathbb{B}\rvert)\\
        0&\leq\lvert\mathbb{A}\cap\mathbb{B}\rvert\leq\min(\lvert\mathbb{A}\rvert,\lvert\mathbb{B}\rvert)
    \end{align*}
    Adding these two inequalities, we get
    \begin{equation*}
        \lvert\mathbb{\mathbb{A}\cup\mathbb{B}}\rvert+\lvert\mathbb{A}\cap\mathbb{B}\rvert\leq(\lvert\mathbb{A}\rvert+\lvert\mathbb{B}\rvert)+\min(\lvert\mathbb{A}\rvert,\lvert\mathbb{B}\rvert)
    \end{equation*}
    Subtracting the nonnegative number, $\lvert\mathbb{A}\rvert+\lvert\mathbb{B}\rvert$, from both sides of the inequality gives us the following inequality:
    \begin{equation*}
        \lvert\mathbb{\mathbb{A}\cup\mathbb{B}}\rvert+\lvert\mathbb{A}\cap\mathbb{B}\rvert-(\lvert\mathbb{A}\rvert+\lvert\mathbb{B}\rvert)\leq\min(\lvert\mathbb{A}\rvert,\lvert\mathbb{B}\rvert)
    \end{equation*}

    \[
        \therefore \lvert\mathbb{\mathbb{A}\cup\mathbb{B}}\rvert+\lvert\mathbb{A}\cap\mathbb{B}\rvert-(\lvert\mathbb{A}\rvert+\lvert\mathbb{B}\rvert)=0,
    \]

    giving 

    \[
        \lvert\mathbb{\mathbb{A}\cup\mathbb{B}}\rvert+\lvert\mathbb{A}\cap\mathbb{B}\rvert=(\lvert\mathbb{A}\rvert+\lvert\mathbb{B}\rvert)
    \]

\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}
    A set with $n$ elements has $2^n$ subsets.
\end{theorem}

\begin{exercise}
    Under the correspondence between numbers and subsets described above, 
    \begin{enumerate}[label={(\alph*)}] 
        \item Which numbers correspond to subsets with $1$ element?
        \item Which number corresponds to the whole set? 
        \item Which sets correspond to even numbers?
    \end{enumerate}
    \label{ex:1.3.1}
\end{exercise}
\textbf{Solution}.
    \begin{enumerate}[label={(\alph*)}] 
        \item In the binary representations of the subsets with $1$ element we must have exactly one $1$. Such representations, owing to the place-value system, correspond to \emph{positive} powers of $2$.
        \item In the binary representation of the subset that is the same as the given set, we must have $n$ $1$'s, which corresponds to $2^n-1$.
        \item The rightmost bit in binary representations of even numbers is 0. This means the \emph{last element} in the preferred order of set elements is not in the corresponding subsets.
    \end{enumerate}

\begin{exercise}
    What is the number of subsets of a set with $n$ elements, containing a given element?
    \label{ex:1.3.2}
\end{exercise}
\textbf{Solution}.
Presence of a `given set element' in any subset is indicated by a 1 at the position representing that element in the binary representation of the corresponding integer. Thus, all but one position (in the binary representations), i.e., $n-1$ positions, can have either a 0 or a 1. From multiplication principle (definition [\ref{def:mp}]), we say that there are such $2^{n-1}$ subsets:
\[
    \underbrace{2\times2\times2\times\dots\times2}_{n-1}=2^{n-1}=\frac{1}{2}\times2^n=2^{n-1}
\]
Exactly half of all the subsets of any nonempty set contain a given element.

\begin{reflection}

    How can we make subsets progressively, for instance, of the $3$-set, $\{a,b,c\}$?

    I first wrote down the subsets of the $2$-set, $\{a,b\}$, as: 
    \[
    \emptyset(\mathbb{E}_0),\{a\} (\mathbb{O}_1),\{b\} (\mathbb{O}_1),\{a,b\}(\mathbb{E}_2)
    \]

    There are $2$ even subsets and 2 odd. 

    Now consider the third element, $c$, of the $3$-set, $\{a,b,c\}$. We can form its subsets from those of the $2$-set above. Let's start with the $\emptyset$ (which is an even set). We can choose to either \emph{merge} $c$ in it or not. This gives two possibilities: $\emptyset$ (an even set) itself, and \emph{the set} $\{c\}$ (an odd set). Thus, one even set begets one even set and one odd set, both subsets of the augmented set.

    How about $\{a\}$, which is an odd set? This gives (not accepting $c$) $\{a\}$ (an odd set) and (accepting $c$) $\{a,c\}$ (an even set).

    $\{b\}$ gives $\{b\}$ and $\{b,c\}$.

    $\{a,b\}$ gives $\{a,b\}$ and $\{a,b,c\}$.

    To sum up,
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item $\mathbb{E}_0$ gives one $\mathbb{E}_0$, one $\mathbb{O}_1$.
        \item $\mathbb{O}_1$ gives one $\mathbb{O}_1$, one $\mathbb{E}_2$.
        \item Another $\mathbb{O}_1$ gives one $\mathbb{O}_1$, one $\mathbb{E}_2$.
        \item $\mathbb{E}_2$ gives one $\mathbb{E}_2$, one $\mathbb{O}_3$.
\end{enumerate}

    Thus, in general, each of the $2^k$ subsets (regardless of whether it's even or odd) of a $k$-set creates $2$ subsets of the $k+1$-set, one even and another odd, effectively doubling the total number of subsets and maintaining the invariant: the number of even sets equals that of the odd sets.
\end{reflection}

\]

\[
    \underbrace{1000\dots0}_{\mathbf{101}}=2^{100}

    We have $20$ \textbf{kinds} of presents; this time, we have a large supply of each kind. We want to give presents to $12$ children. Again, it is not required that every child get\footnote{The mood is subjunctive, where the base form of the verb is used. The book most probably has a typo; it uses `gets' here. \end{exercise}

This time, we have unlimited balls of each of $n$ \emph{kinds}\footnote{A \emph{kind} can be realized most effectively by numbering, e.g. the $k_1$ balls are all numbered $1$, $k_2$ numbered $2$, and so on. Another way to realize them is by the way of colors.}. We try to find $F(n+1,m)$ in terms of $F(n,m)$, where $F(x,y)$ denotes the number of ways of placing balls of $x$ distinct kinds in $m$ urns such that no urn has more than 1 ball of \emph{the same kind}\footnote{Combinatorial problems is about constraints such as these. We must model the problems correctly and satisfy those constraints!}.

After we have placed $n$ balls in $m$ urns the way we must, consider any placement like below:
    \[
        \overbrace{
            \underbrace{\underbrace{|\quad|}_{u_1}\underbrace{|b_1|}_{u_2}\underbrace{|b_2|}_{u_3}\dots\underbrace{|\dots,b_n|}_{u_m}}_{m\text{ urns (no two balls of the \emph{same kind} in any urn)}}
    }^{\text{balls of at most } n\text{ kinds}}
    \]

Here, $b_i$ means a ball of $i^{th}$ \emph{kind}, not $i^{th}$ ball; we have only ordered the kinds of balls, not the balls themselves.

Unlimited number of a new kind of ball, $b_{n+1}$, become available. We must place any number of balls of this kind in the same $m$ urns without violating the constraint that no two $b_{n+1}$'s may be placed in the same urn. Of course, no $b_{n+1}$ has been placed any of our placements so far.

A subtle difference\footnote{This became clear after a discussion with Deepa Joshi. Earlier, I was not considering an $F(n,m)$ to also be an $F(n+1,m)$.} between the previous problem (exercise [\ref{ex:1.5.5}]) and this one is that here every $F(n,m)$ placement is also a valid placement for $F(n+1,m)$, because a placement of $n$ kinds of ball in $m$ urns such that no urn gets two or more balls of the same kind is also a placement of $n+1$ kinds of ball in $m$ urns (with the same constraint). In the previous problem, we had to ensure that we place all the $n+1$ balls in $m$ urns for $F(n+1,m)$.

We make two equivalent arguments:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item An additive argument: We can certainly choose to place one $b_{n+1}$ in any one of the $m$ urns, which can be done in $\binom{m}{1}$ ways. We can place a $b_{n+1}$ in any two of the $m$ urns, which can be done in $\binom{m}{2}$ ways, and so on. The total number of ways of doing this for all the urns is

        \[
            \binom{m}{1}+\binom{m}{2}+\dots+\binom{m}{m}=\sum_{i=1}^m\binom{m}{i}
        \]
        ($i$ does not assume $0$, because that means we are not placing a ball of a new kind in any urn.)
    \item A `binary' argument: We have $m$ urns. We either place a ball of new kind in an urn or not. This is analogous to creating binary $m$-strings with the exception of the $m$-string: $\underbrace{\mathtt{00\dots0}}_{\mathbf{m}}$. The number of such binary $m$-strings, and hence the number of placements, is $2^m-1$.
\end{enumerate}

It turns out that the sum in item 1) above evaluates to the number in item 2). 

Thus, each placement of $n$ kinds of balls in $m$ urns creates $2^m-1$ placements of $n+1$ kinds. This sets up the following recurrence:

    \begin{align}
        F(0,m)&=1\nonumber\\
        F(n+1,m)=F(n,m)+(2^m-1)\times F(n,m)&=2^m\times F(n,m)
        \label{eq:rec-n-kinds-of-ball-m-urns}
    \end{align}
The solution of the recurrence in equation [\ref{eq:rec-n-kinds-of-ball-m-urns}] is
\[
    F(n,m)=2^{mn}
\]
    Therefore, the total number of ways to distribute $20$ \emph{different kinds of presents} among $12$ children so that no child receives more than 1 present of the same kind is 
    \[
        F(20,12)=(2^{12})^{20}=2^{240}
    \]

\section{Permutations}
\begin{definition}[Permutation of Objects]
    If we have a \textbf{list} of $n$ objects (an \textbf{ordered set}, where it is specified which element is the first, second, etc.), and we rearrange them so that they are in another order, this is called \textbf{permuting} them; the new order is called a permutation of the objects. 

    We also call the rearrangement that does not change anything a permutation.
\end{definition}
\begin{example}
    $n!=n\times(n-1)\times(n-2)\times\dots\times2\times1$ is the number of ways to order $n$ objects.
\end{example}

\begin{theorem}
The number of permutations of $n$ objects is $n!$.
    \label{th:n-perms}
\end{theorem}

\begin{reflection}[The Decision Tree Based Exposition]
    I like how the authors contrast two combinatorial objects (number of subsets of an $n$-set and the number of permutations of $n$ objects) using a `decision tree.' In either case, \textbf{the combinatorial object we seek is found at a leaf of the decision tree}.
    \begin{enumerate}
    \item While thinking of subsets of an $n$-set, we ask if each of the set elements is in the subset we are making. In this case, our decision tree is always an $n$-level `binary tree'. The number of leaves is $2^n$. Each leaf is a subset.
    \item While thinking of permutations of $n$ objects, we assume some order and ask which is the first element, second element, and so on. In this case, our decision tree is always an $n$-level `level tree' with $n$ nodes at the first level, $n\times(n-1)$ nodes at the second level, and so on. The number of leaves is $n!$. Each leaf is a permutation.
    \end{enumerate}
\end{reflection}
\begin{exercise}
    \label{ex:1.6.1}
    $n$ boys and $n$ girls go out to dance. In how many ways can they all dance simultaneously? (We assume that only couples of mixed gender dance with each other.)
\end{exercise}
\textbf{Solution}.
An inductive argument comes to mind.

Let there be $n$ boys and $n$ girls such that a boy pairs up with a girl to dance and all the $n$ pairs dance simultaneously. We denote the boys from $b_1$ to $b_n$ and girls from $g_1$ to $g_n$. 

With $n$ boys and $n$ girls, a `dance pairing' has exactly $n$ boy-girl pairs like $b_i-g_j$. For $n=2$, we have the following two dance pairings:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item $\{b_1-g_1,b_2-g_2\}$
    \item $\{b_1-g_2,b_2-g_1\}$
\end{enumerate}

Consider an arbitrary dance pairing of $n$ boys and $n$ girls:
\[
    \{\underbrace{b_1-g_2,b_2-g_3,\dots,b_n-g_m}_{n\text{ boy-girl pairs}}\}
\]

A new boy, $b_{n+1}$, and a new girl, $g_{n+1}$, arrive. We can pair up $b_{n+1}$ with any of $g_1,g_2,\dots,g_{n},g_{n+1}$. There are $n+1$ choices. Then $g_{n+1}$ would pair up with $b_{n+1}$'s dancing partner's previous dancing partner, if any. This creates exactly $n+1$ new dance pairings from one previous dance pairing. For example, if $b_{n+1}$ is paired up with $g_2$ from the above dance pairing, $g_{n+1}$ would be paired up with $b_1$. Then, we get a new dance pairing:
\[
    \{\underbrace{b_{n+1}-g_2,b_2-g_3,\dots,b_n-g_m,b_1-g_{n+1}}_{n+1\text{ boy-girl pairs}}\}
\]

If $D(n)$ denotes the total number of dance pairings with $n$ boys and $n$ girls, then,
\begin{equation}
    D(n+1)=(n+1)\times D(n)
    \label{eq:dance-pairings}
\end{equation}

The solution of equation [\ref{eq:dance-pairings}] is
\[
    D(n)=n!
\]

\begin{reflection}[An Arguably Simpler Argument]
    The above inductive argument seems correct. However, there is another argument based on permutations\footnote{Deepa Joshi formulated this argument in a conversation.}.

    We imagine that the dance floor comprises `dance stations,' like, for instance, chessboards. $g_1$ comes to the first dance station. She has $n$ choices of a boy partner. After they pair up, the next girl, $g_2$, comes to the next dance station. She only has $n-1$ choices for a boy partner. Together, they have $n\times(n-1)$ choices regardless of the choices made by the rest. Continuing further, $g_{n-1}$ only has two choices. Therefore, from either the theorem [\ref{th:n-perms}] or the multiplication principle (definition [\ref{def:mp}]), the total number of dance pairings (which is what the exercise asks for) is $n!$.
\end{reflection}

\section{The Number of `Ordered' k-subsets of an n-set}
This section introduces the idea of an `ordered' subset (or ordered set). How can a set be ordered, aren't $\{2,1\}$ and $\{1,2\}$ the same set? Right, they are the same set, but not the same ordered set. Simply speaking, an ordered set is a way to order the elements of a set according to an ordering rule. If no such rule exists, the set cannot be ordered. Fortunately, many sets are ordered.

By extension, when we impose an order on the subsets of a set, we get its ordered subsets. We use the pair of parentheses to denote an ordered set\footnote{The $\langle\rangle$ is used for sequences, which are different.}.
\begin{example}[Ordered $2$-subsets of $\{1,2,3\}$]
    Here are the two ordered $2$-subsets of $\{1,2,3\}$:
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item $(1,2)$
        \item $(2,1)$
    \end{enumerate}
    The ordering criterion decides which set or subset is considered before others. Thus,
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item $(2,1)$
        \item $(1,2)$
    \end{enumerate}

    are also ordered $2$-subsets of the given set.

    The number of ordered sets created by ordering all the elements of an $n$-set is $n!$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[A Competition]
At a competition of $100$ athletes, only the order of the first $10$ is recorded.  How many different outcomes does the competition have?

This question can be answered along the lines of the arguments we have seen. The first place can be won by any of the athletes; no matter who wins, there are $99$ possible second place winners, so the first two prizes can go $100\times99$ ways. Given the first two, there are $98$ athletes who can be third, etc. So the answer is $100\times99\times\dots\times91$.
\end{example}

        \item \label{item:rb}Once we have chosen one outcome for the $10$ best athletes, the next best, $11^{th}$, athlete can be chosen $90$ ways, and the next after that $89$ ways, and so on.             \]

\begin{theorem}[The Number of Ordered $k$-subsets of an $n$-set]
    An $n$-set has
    \[
        n\times(n-1)\times(n-2)\times\dots\times(n-k+1)
    \]
    \textbf{ordered} $k$-subsets.
    \label{th:ordered-k-subsets}
\end{theorem}

[\ref{th:ordered-k-subsets}] is an important theorem! The following follows from it: 

Consider an $n$-set. Then,

The number of \emph{ordered} $n$-sets= The number of their \emph{ordered} $k$-subsets $\times$ The number of $n-k$ objects, that is,
\[
    n!=\big(n\times(n-1)\times(n-2)\times\dots\times(n-k+2)\times(n-k+1)\big)\times(n-k)!
\]

Alternatively, the number of \emph{ordered} $k$-subsets of an $n$-set is $\frac{n!}{(n-k)!}$.

\section{The Number of k-subsets of an n-set}
\begin{theorem}
    \label{th:n-choose-k}
    It follows from theorem [\ref{th:ordered-k-subsets}] that, the number of $k$-subsets\footnote{Not `ordered.'} of an $n$-set is
    \begin{equation}
        \binom{n}{k}=\frac{n\times(n-1)\times\dots\times(n-k+1)}{k!}=\frac{n!}{k!(n-k)!}
        \label{eq:n-choose-k}
    \end{equation}
\end{theorem}
This is because the number of permutations (orders) of a $k$-set is $k!$.


The number of $k$-subsets of an $n$-set is such an important quantity that there is a special notation for it: $\dbinom{n}{k}$ (read ``n choose k'').

\[

\]
\[
    \binom{n}{1}=\frac{n!}{1!\times(n-1)!}=\frac{n\times(n-1)!}{1\times(n-1)!}=n=\binom{n}{n-1}
\]
While forming a $k$-subset of an $n$-set, we can either explicitly choose $k$ elements($\binom{n}{k}$), or reject $n-k$ elements ($\binom{n}{n-k}$). Therefore,
\[
    \binom{n}{k}=\binom{n}{n-k}\forall 0\leq k\leq n
\]

\begin{theorem}[Properties of Binomial Coefficients]
    \label{th:binomial-coeffs-identities}
    Binomial coefficients (numbers $\binom{n}{k}\forall k,n\geq0, k\leq n$ for various values of $k$ for a given value of $n$) satisfy the following identities:

    \begin{align}
        \binom{n}{k}&=\binom{n}{n-k} \label{eq:nck=ncn-k}\\
        \binom{n-1}{k-1}+\binom{n-1}{k}&=\binom{n}{k} \forall n,k>0\label{eq:n-1ck-1}\\
        \binom{n}{0}+\binom{n}{1}+\binom{n}{2}+\dots+\binom{n}{n-1}+\binom{n}{n}&=2^n\label{eq:bin-coeffs-sum}
    \end{align}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
    A combinatorial proof for equation [\ref{eq:nck=ncn-k}] is elegant. We show a 1:1 correspondence between the $k$-subset and $n-k$-subset of an $n$-set.

    A combinatorial proof for equation [\ref{eq:n-1ck-1}] is harder, I guess. So, algebraic manipulation is used. First, if $n$ equals $k$,
    \[
        \binom{n-1}{n-1}+\binom{n-1}{n}=\binom{n}{n}
    \]
    holds, because $\binom{n-1}{n-1}$,$\binom{n}{n}$ are both $1$ and $\binom{n-1}{n}$ is $0$ (because there is no $n$-subset of an $(n-1)$-set). 

    When $k<n$, the following holds:
    \[
        \frac{n}{k(n-k)}=\frac{1}{k}+\frac{1}{n-k}
    \]
    We can multiply both sides by $(n-1)!$ and divide by $(k-1)!(n-k-1)!$ to get it in the coveted form:
    \[
        \frac{n(n-1)!}{k(k-1)!(n-k)(n-k-1)!}=\frac{(n-1)!}{k(k-1)!(n-k-1)!}+\frac{(n-1)!}{k!(n-k)(n-k-1)!}
    \]
    or,
    \[
        \frac{n!}{k!(n-k)!}=\frac{(n-1)!}{k!(n-k-1)!}+\frac{(n-1)!}{(k-1)!(n-k)!}
    \]
    that is,
    \[
        \frac{n!}{k!(n-k)!}=\frac{(n-1)!}{k!((n-1)-k)!}+\frac{(n-1)!}{(k-1)!((n-1)-(k-1))!}
    \]
    or,
    \[
        \binom{n-1}{k-1}+\binom{n-1}{k}=\binom{n}{k}
    \]
\end{proof}
    \begin{reflection}
        \label{ref:n-1ck-1}
        I like this argument for the equation [\ref{eq:n-1ck-1}] better than given in the book. The book assumes what needs to be proved and reaches a tautology.

        What can be a `combinatorial argument' here? Translating into words this identity means the following:
        \begin{leftbar}
            The sum of the number of $k$-subsets and the number of $(k-1)$-subsets of an $(n-1)$-set equals the number of $k$-subsets of an $n$-set.
        \end{leftbar}

        However, that is not very helpful, is it?

        After a while, a thought occurred to me when I focused on $\binom{n}{k}$. An argument emerged as follows.
        \begin{proof}
            $\binom{n}{k}$ is the number of $k$-subsets of an $n$-set. Let's say a new element is added to the $n$-set, making it an $(n+1)$-set. 

        What are $k$-subsets of the new $(n+1)$-set?

        All the $k$-subsets of the previous $n$-set are also the $k$-subsets of the new $(n+1)$-set. The new element can also be added to make every $(k-1)$-subset of the previous $n$-set so that it becomes the $k$-subset of the new $(n+1)$-set. There is no other $k$-subset of the new $(n+1)$-set. Therefore,
        \[
            \binom{n}{k}+\binom{n}{k-1}=\binom{n+1}{k}
        \]
        Setting $n=m-1$ restates the same result in the desired form:
            \[
                \binom{m-1}{k}+\binom{m-1}{k-1}=\binom{m}{k}
            \]
        \end{proof}
    \end{reflection}
Finally, we prove equation [\ref{eq:bin-coeffs-sum}] through the combinatorial interpretation again.  Again, let $\mathbb{S}$ be an $n$-element set. The first term on the left-hand side counts the $0$-element subsets of $\mathbb{S}$ (there is only one, the empty set); the second term counts $1$-element subsets; the next term, $2$-element subsets, etc. In the whole sum, every subset of $\mathbb{S}$ is counted exactly once. We know that $2^n$ (the right-hand side) is the number of all subsets of $\mathbb{S}$. This proves [\ref{eq:bin-coeffs-sum}], and completes the proof of theorem [\ref{th:binomial-coeffs-identities}].

    \label{ex:1.8.14}
    Three sets have $5$, $10$, and $15$ elements, respectively. How many elements can their union and their intersection have?
\end{exercise}
\textbf{Solution}.
Let $\lvert\mathbb{A}\rvert=5,\lvert\mathbb{B}\rvert=10,\lvert\mathbb{C}\rvert=15$.

Let's think of their union, $\mathbb{D}=\mathbb{A}\cup\mathbb{B}\cup\mathbb{C}$, first. 

$\mathbb{D}$ must have at least $15$ elements (when $(\mathbb{A}\subset\mathbb{C})\land(\mathbb{B}\subset\mathbb{C})$) and at most $5+10+15=30$ elements (when $\mathbb{A}\cap\mathbb{B}\cap\mathbb{C}=\emptyset$). Then, we replace one element in $\mathbb{A}$ with an element that is \emph{only} in $\mathbb{A}$. In that case, $\mathbb{D}$ has $1+15=16$ elements. Therefore, $15\leq\lvert\mathbb{D}\rvert\leq30$.

For their intersection, $\mathbb{E}=\mathbb{A}\cap\mathbb{B}\cap\mathbb{C}$, we can start with disjoint sets. Then, $\mathbb{E}=\emptyset$ and $\lvert\mathbb{E}\rvert=0$; we replace an element of all with one element common to all and $\lvert\mathbb{E}\rvert=1$. Gradually $\lvert\mathbb{E}\rvert$ rises, but only up to $5$, when $\mathbb{A}$ is contained in both $\mathbb{B},\mathbb{C}$. Therefore, $0\leq\lvert\mathbb{E}\rvert\leq5$.

\begin{exercise}
    \label{ex:1.8.21}
    Let $\mathbb{B}$ be a subset of $\mathbb{A}$, $\lvert\mathbb{A}\rvert=n$, and $\lvert\mathbb{B}\rvert=k$. What is the number of all subsets of A whose intersection with $\mathbb{B}$ has $1$ element?
\end{exercise}
\textbf{Solution}.
If we represent the sets by circles, since $\mathbb{B}\subset\mathbb{A}$, the circle representing $\mathbb{B}$ is \emph{inside (or coincides with)} the circle representing $\mathbb{A}$. To satisfy our condition, exactly one element of any subset of $\mathbb{A}$ must be in $\mathbb{B}$. 

How many $1$-subsets of $\mathbb{A}$ share $1$ element with $\mathbb{B}$? Clearly, each element of $\mathbb{B}$ (which can be chosen $k\times\dbinom{n-k}{0}=k$ ways, since $\lvert\mathbb{B}\rvert=k$ and we choose no element from the remaining $n-k$). 

How many $2$-subsets of $\mathbb{A}$ have that property? The subsets for which one element is in $\mathbb{B}$ and one in the set $\mathbb{A}\setminus\mathbb{B}$. The number of $2$-subsets is, therefore, equal to the number of ways to choose the two elements together, that is, $k\times(n-k)$ or $k\times\dbinom{n-k}{1}$.

The $3$-subsets satisfying that condition are the ones for which one element is in $\mathbb{B}$ and two in the set $\mathbb{A}\setminus\mathbb{B}$. That number is $k\times\dbinom{n-k}{2}$. 

This continues up to the $(n-1)$-subsets of $\mathbb{A}$. Therefore, the total number of subsets of $\mathbb{A}$ with one element in $\mathbb{B}$, $s_{\scriptscriptstyle 1B}$ is
\begin{align*}
    s_{\scriptscriptstyle 1B}&=k\times\dbinom{n-k}{0}+k\times\dbinom{n-k}{1}+k\times\dbinom{n-k}{2}+\dots+k\times\dbinom{n-k}{n-k}\\
    &\text{(since there are $n-k$ elements in $\mathbb{A}\setminus\mathbb{B}$)}\\
    &=k\times\sum_{i=0}^{n-k}\dbinom{n-k}{i}\\
    &=k\times2^{n-k}
\end{align*}

\begin{proof}
We \emph{write} a natural number ($a$) in the \emph{place-value system} of any base greater than 1 ($b$) by repeatedly dividing $a$ by $b$. From the division algorithm, it follows that for any two integers $a,b\;(b\ne0)$, there exist two integers $q, r$, called quotient and remainder respectively, such that
\[
    a=bq+r\land0\leq r<b
\]

On the first division, we get $a=bq_1+r_1\;(0\leq r_1<b)$. Then we write $r_1$.

If $q_1$ is larger than $b$, we perform another division to get $q_1=bq_2+r_2$, or $a=b(bq_2+r_2)+r_1=b^2q_2+br_2+r_1\;(0\leq r_1,r_2<b)$. Then we write $r_2$ to the left of $r_1$. So far, we have written two `digits' both less than $b$: $r_2r_1$ and we need to `process the remains' in $q_2$.

It follows that the quotient $q_i$ become smaller with each division. We stop the process when a certain $q_i$ becomes less than $b$, after which all $q_i$ are $0$. This is the digit we finally write. The \emph{value} of our number is
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:place-value-val}
    a=b^{n-1}q_{n}+b^{n-2}r_{n-1}+\dots+b^2r_3+br_2+r_1
\end{equation}
and we \emph{write} it as\footnote{In practice, we usually start writing from the big end, i.e., $q_n$ in this case.}:
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:place-value-rep}
    q_nr_{n-1}\dots r_3r_2r_1
\end{equation}

Each of the digits in equation [\ref{eq:place-value-rep}] is less than $b$. This means that when $b=2$, each of the digits in equation [\ref{eq:place-value-rep}] is either $0$ or $1$. It then follows that the value of the number given by equation [\ref{eq:place-value-val}] is a sum of powers of $2$.

    From the multiplication principle (definition [\ref{def:mp}]), it follows that if we have $n$ binary digits (bits), we can choose them together $2^n$ \emph{distinct ways}: each representation (a binary $n$-string) differs from every other representation in at least one bit. Thus, the set of $2^n$ binary representations and the set of numbers each binary representation represents form a bijection (a 1:1 mapping). This proves that every binary representation uniquely represents an integer.
\end{proof}
\begin{exercise}
    \label{ex:1.8.24}
    How many bits does $10^{100}$ have if written in base $2$?
\end{exercise}
\textbf{Solution}.
If we have $n$ \emph{bits} to write the \emph{binary} representation of a number, the minimum value of that number occurs when the most significant bit is 1 and other bits are zero:
\[
    \mathtt{1\underbrace{000\dots0}_{\text{$n-1$ bits}}}:\quad1\times2^{n-1}+\underbrace{0\times2^{n-2}+\dots+0\times2^1+0\times2^0}_{n-1\text{ bits}}=2^{n-1}
\]
and the maximum value occurs when \emph{all} bits are $1$:
\[
    \mathtt{\underbrace{1111\dots1}_{\text{$n$ bits}}}:\quad\underbrace{1\times2^{n-1}+1\times2^{n-2}+\dots+1\times2^1+1\times2^0}_{n\text{ bits}}=2^n-1
\]

Therefore, the number that we want to represent as a binary string, $10^{100}$, must be between these two limits:
\[
    2^{n-1}\leq10^{100}\leq(2^n-1)
\]
or, equivalently,
\[
    2^{n-1}\leq10^{100}<2^n
\]
Taking logarithms to the base $2$, we get
\begin{align*}
    n-1&\leq100\times\log_2{10}<n\\
    n-1&\leq100\times3.321928094887362<n\\
    n-1&\leq332.1928094887362<n\\
    \therefore n&=333
\end{align*}

\begin{exercise}
    \label{ex:1.8.26}
    Find the number of all $\mathit{20}$-digit integers in which no two consecutive digits are the same.
\end{exercise}
\textbf{Solution}.
There are $9$ choices ($1,2,3,\dots,\text{ or },9$) for the most significant digit of an $n$-digit integer. For every other digit, however, there are $10$ choices ($0,1,2,\dots,\text{ or },9$). In a number $C$ that has no consecutive pair, after we choose a first digit (say $3$), we may not choose $3$ for the second digit (but we may choose $3$ for any subsequent digit). That leaves only $9$ choices for the second digit. The number of choices for subsequent digits also remains $9$. Thus, the digits of $C$ can be chosen together
\[
    \underbrace{9\times9\times9\times\dots\times9}_{n\text{ digits }}=9^n
\]
ways.

Therefore, the number of $20$-digit numbers with no consecutive pair of the same digit is $9^{20}$.
\begin{reflection}[Two Ways]
    The above argument sounds correct. However, I am a bit apprehensive. I feel the need of solving this problem another way. Addressing this apprehension is why Lov\'asz et.al. want us to solve combinatorial problems two different ways.

    Solving it another way is somewhat challenging. Let's think of $4$-digit decimal numbers. There are $9\times10\times10\times10=9000$ of them. How many of them have at least one consecutive pair of a digit (e.g. $1128$)? Directly counting them by fixing a same-digit-consecutive-pair in various positions and counting choices for the remaining digits feels tricky in the general case. There is an overcounting trap laid by numbers like $1111$ or $2244$.

    Will an inductive argument help? For example, there are $9$ numbers with a same-digit-consecutive-pair in $2$-digit numbers:$11,22,33,44,55,66,77,88,99$. How can we use that fact to ascertain the $3$-digit numbers with a same-digit-consecutive-pair? In the range $100$-$199$, there are $100$, $(110,111,112,\dots,119$, and $(122,133,\dots,188,199)$, that is, $1+10+8=19$. This holds for every hundred up to $900$-$999$. That gives us $19\times9=171$ such numbers for the $3$-digit case. However, I don't see a pattern yet.
\end{reflection}
